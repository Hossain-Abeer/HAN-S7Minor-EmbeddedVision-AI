1. ESSENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
L2 answers the question "what physical chain turns a scene into pixel values,
and what knobs does each link expose?" Five links: illumination, optical
system (lens + filters), camera (sensor + shutter + ADC + interface), frame
grabber / communication bus, and computer + software. Every image-processing
algorithm in L3–L14 silently assumes this chain has already delivered a
sampled, quantized, correctly focused, evenly illuminated image. L2 tells you
how each of those assumptions can fail — and what numeric parameters
(pixel pitch, fill factor, dynamic range, focal length, working distance,
FOV, MTF/Nyquist) quantify the failure.

2.1  BASIC TERMINOLOGY — the five-link chain and its four defining distances
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 3: "Hardware" block diagram]
  Illumination  →  Optical system (lens)  →  Camera  →  Sensor  →
  Computer
  These are the five physical subsystems. Sensor is drawn outside the
  camera box in the slide because it lives inside the camera but is the
  element that decides most of the performance.

[L2 slide 4: "Hardware" — four distances]
  Four geometric parameters define every machine-vision setup:

    Depth of field (focus range)
        Distance between the NEAREST and FARTHEST object planes that
        appear acceptably sharp. Also called focus range.

    Working distance (WD)
        Distance from the FRONT of the LENS to the object under inspection.
        Some lenses (objectives) have a FIXED working distance; most
        machine-vision lenses give a working-distance RANGE.

    Resolution
        The MINIMUM FEATURE SIZE of the object that the system can resolve.
        Drives camera + lens selection because it constrains accuracy and
        repeatability of every downstream measurement.
        Units: mm, µm — or (equivalently) line-pairs / mm of the *image*.

    Field of view (FOV)
        The VIEWABLE AREA of the object. Portion of the object that fills
        the camera sensor.

  Figure to look up: [L2 slide 4 / syllabus Figure 4]
    "Overview of most relevant distances in a camera setup"
    (source: http://machine-vision-mag.com/industry/machine-vision-
     fundamentals/). This diagram is the canonical picture of DoF, WD,
    resolution, and FOV in one figure.

2.3  IMAGE SENSORS — CCD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slides 6–7: "Image sensors"]
  Two dominant sensor families:
    CCD  = Charge-Coupled Device
    CMOS = Complementary Metal-Oxide Semiconductor
  Plus special-purpose sensing:
    Infrared, X-ray, Radar, MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging), Stereo vision

[L2 slide 8: "Spectral sensitivity"]
  The slide superimposes three curves:
    --- Human eye
    - - CMOS
    - - - - CCD
  Purpose: show that all three respond to visible light but with different
  roll-offs into IR and UV. That is why "which sensor for which band"
  (§2.5) is a real design question, not a preference.

[L2 slide 9: "Image sensors — CCD" (structure)]
  Longitudinal cross-section of a single CCD cell:
    Metal gate
    Insulator (silicon oxide)
    Φ1  Φ2  Φ3   (three clock phases above the silicon)
    P+ silicon
    A depleted photo-sensitive area with free charges under the gate
    (charge accumulates here in response to incoming photons)

  Mechanism (WHY): CCD stores photocharge under a gate, then SHIFTS it
  along the row by pulsing Φ1, Φ2, Φ3 in a bucket-brigade pattern until it
  reaches the output amplifier. That is the "charge-coupled" in the name.

[L2 slide 10: "Full frame CCD"]
  - Pixels are exposed DURING readout → smearing.
  - A mechanical shutter or a strobe light can solve smearing.
  - Close to 100% fill factor → high SNR.

  "Full frame" means the sensor has no shielded storage area; the entire
  array is photosensitive. That is why smearing occurs: while charge is
  being shifted out, photons are still landing on the very cells being
  read.

[L2 slide 11: "CCD — limited charge"]
  - Elements have a limited charge capacity.
  - If exposed too long, charge overflows to neighbouring pixels → BLOOMING.

  Blooming is the CCD-specific failure that CMOS largely avoids because
  each CMOS pixel has its own amplifier and reset before overflow.

[L2 slide 12: "Frame transfer CCD"]
  - Reduces smearing by a very quick (<500 µs) transfer of collected
    charges into a SHIELDED area.
  - Close to 100% fill factor.
  - High cost, because the shielded area is large silicon real estate.

  Trade-off (memorise): full-frame is cheap but smears; frame-transfer is
  smear-free but roughly doubles sensor area and price. Interline transfer
  (not on the slide but standard) sacrifices fill factor to put a shielded
  column next to every photosensitive column — the opposite trade.

[L2 slide 13: "CCD — blooming diagram"]
  Image-only slide. Caption: charge spills from a saturated well into
  neighbours. Look up by the caption "Image sensors — CCD" on slide 13.

2.4  IMAGE SENSORS — CMOS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 14: "Image sensors — CMOS" (architecture)]
  Vertical column- and row-addressing similar to RAM.
    Sequencer → Row addressing → Vertical column bus →
    Column amplifier → Analog multiplexer → ADC → Horizontal addressing

  Consequences shown on the same slide:
    • Low fill factor → low SNR (architectural overhead: transistors
      occupy silicon that would otherwise be photosensitive).
    • Readout logic can be implemented ON THE SAME CHIP (this is why CMOS
      is cheap and easy to integrate).
    • Random access of pixels enables:
        - region of interest (ROI) readout — window the sensor;
        - balancing temporal vs spatial resolution (more fps at fewer rows).

  Mechanism (WHY): each CMOS pixel contains its own photodiode PLUS a
  source-follower amplifier AND a reset transistor. That is several
  transistors per pixel, taking up area. In return, the signal is converted
  to a voltage immediately (and often to bits right there), and every pixel
  can be addressed independently.

2.6  BAYER FILTER (single-chip colour)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 7: "Bayer filter" — 4-step diagram]
  Four-stage picture:
    1. Original scene (colour)
    2. Output sensor with Bayer filter — each pixel sees only R, G, or B
    3. Output colour-coded with Bayer filter colours — 2×2 repeating R/G/G/B
    4. Reconstructed image after interpolating — the missing two channels
       of each pixel are synthesised from neighbours

  Mechanism (WHY): a monochrome silicon sensor cannot detect colour. The
  Bayer filter puts a coloured microlens above every pixel so each pixel
  sees one channel. Demosaicing then interpolates the two missing channels
  per pixel. Two thirds of the raw data is an interpolation — that is why
  single-chip colour cameras have lower effective spatial resolution than
  the pixel count suggests.

  Anti-aliasing consequence (links to L1 §2.4): the Bayer colour grid is a
  spatial sampler at 2×2 pixel period; fine high-frequency scene patterns
  can produce COLOUR MOIRÉ (false colour) even if the intensity Nyquist
  limit is met.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.7  SHUTTERS — global vs rolling
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slides 15–16: "Image sensors — Shutters"]
  Purpose: control the START and STOP of exposure.
  Types: mechanical OR electronic.
  Electronic control uses two signals: SAMPLE and RESET.

  GLOBAL SHUTTER
    - Sample and reset applied to ALL pixels at the SAME time.
    - The frame represents one instant in time.
    - NO motion artefacts from moving subjects.

  ROLLING SHUTTER
    - Sequential control of exposure with interleaved readout (row by row).
    - Advantage: the sensor continues to gather photons while rows are
      being read out → effectively higher sensitivity.
    - Found on most CMOS cameras.
    - Disadvantage: motion artefacts — a fast-moving object appears
      sheared / bent / tilted because different rows were exposed at
      different times.

  CCD cameras typically use GLOBAL shutters (they take a snapshot of a
  single instant in time) and therefore do not suffer rolling-shutter
  artefacts.

  Numeric anchor: full-frame video frame time = 1/30 s. A car crossing the
  frame at 30 m/s during that frame advances ~1 m; under a rolling shutter
  the top row and bottom row record positions ~1 m apart → visible skew.

2.8  IMAGE SENSOR NOISE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slides 17–18: "Noise" — three-level taxonomy]
  Two families, each with sub-causes:

  TEMPORAL NOISE  (varies frame to frame)
    Photon noise
      Shot noise — Poisson statistical noise in photon arrival.
      Background noise — photons not from the object.
    Thermal noise
      Dark current and amplifier noise.
    Mitigation: averaging over time reduces temporal noise by 1/sqrt(N)
    for N frames.

  SPATIAL NOISE  (fixed pattern across the frame)
    Sensitivity variations
      Each pixel's gain differs slightly (photoresponsive nonuniformity).
    Dark signal variations
      Each pixel's dark current differs; typically grows with temperature.
    Related to the MANUFACTURING process — called pattern noise.

  Practical meaning:
    Temporal noise → treated by mean filters or frame averaging (L9).
    Spatial (pattern) noise → cannot be removed by averaging; needs flat-
    field correction (divide by a uniform-field reference — L4).

2.9  CCD vs CMOS PERFORMANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 19: "Image sensors — Performance CCD vs CMOS" (SNR graph)]
  Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) plotted vs input light level.
  Two curves: --- CCD, --- CMOS.
  Crossover at very low light (~4 photons / pixel).
  Caption (verbatim): "CCD is best choice for extremely low light
  application (< 4 photons/pixel)".
  Source: Bradly S. Carlson, "Evaluating image sensor sensitivity by
  measuring camera signal-to-noise ratio".

  Interpretation: at any useful illumination level, CMOS is competitive
  or better. Only at the extreme photon-starved corner does CCD win. That
  is why CCD survives only in astronomy, some scientific imaging, and
  legacy industrial lines, while consumer and most machine vision has moved
  to CMOS.

2.10  FILL FACTOR AND MICROLENSES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 20: "Fill factor" — microlenses]
  Definition
    Fill factor = (photosensitive area) / (total pixel area).
    Range: 0–1, commonly 30–70% for frontside-illuminated CMOS,
    approaching 100% for full-frame CCD or backside-illuminated CMOS.

  Microlenses
    - Small lenses above each pixel that focus incident light onto the
      photosensitive region (which is smaller than the whole pixel).
    - Increase effective fill factor without enlarging the photodiode.
    - Used with interline-transfer CCDs and CMOS sensors.

  Why it matters: low fill factor = low SNR (less light per pixel). The
  L2 text on CMOS (§2.4) explicitly says "low fill factor → low SNR".

  Numeric anchor: a CMOS pixel with 30% fill factor and pixel pitch 3 µm
  has photodiode area ~2.7 µm². A CCD pixel at 95% fill factor and pitch
  3 µm has photodiode area ~8.6 µm² — about 3× more light captured per
  pixel per exposure.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.11  READ-OUT MODES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 21: "Read out modes" + interlaced/progressive diagram]
  Two ways to scan a sensor into frames:

  INTERLACED
    - A frame is read out as TWO fields: odd rows (odd field) then even
      rows (even field).
    - Field frequency = 2× frame frequency → less perceived flicker.
    - Odd and even fields are exposed at slightly different times →
      motion artefacts for moving objects.

  PROGRESSIVE
    - All rows scanned sequentially.
    - One exposure per frame; single-instant snapshot.
    - Preferred for machine vision and any motion-critical application.

  Figure to look up: [L2 slide 21] "Interlaced / Progressive" pair of
  diagrams showing the scan path in each mode.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.12  ACQUISITION MODES  (driver ↔ application timing)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slides 22–24: "Acquisition modes"]
  Three patterns, distinguished by WHEN the driver grabs the image and WHEN
  the application processes it:

  SYNCHRONOUS from a free-running camera
    - Driver grab and application process are SEQUENTIAL.
    - Speed limit: processing speed < HALF frame speed.
    - Every other frame is effectively lost because grab and process
      cannot overlap.

  ASYNCHRONOUS from a free-running camera
    - Grab and process are INTERLEAVED (double-buffering).
    - Speed limit: processing speed ≤ FRAME speed.
    - This is the normal pipelined mode in modern SDKs.

  TRIGGERED ASYNCHRONOUS from an asynchronously-reset ("snap-shot") camera
    - External trigger resets the sensor at the exact moment the object of
      interest is in view.
    - Grab and process are INTERLEAVED as in asynchronous mode.
    - Speed limit: processing speed ≤ FRAME speed.
    - Trigger source can be a photo-eye, a PLC signal, or software.

  The three slides are summarised in the exam as "processing speed versus
  frame speed" — remember the "< half" vs "≤ frame" distinction.

2.13  HIGH-SPEED AND MICRO CAMERAS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 25: "High speed camera"]
  Ultrahigh-speed specs stated on the slide:
    - Up to 25 000 fps at 1-megapixel resolution.
    - Up to 1 000 000 fps at reduced resolution.

  Mechanism (WHY): frame rate is limited by readout bandwidth and by
  exposure time. At fixed bandwidth, higher fps forces smaller row count
  (fewer pixels per frame). So "1 M fps at reduced resolution" means
  roughly (bandwidth) / (1 M pixels/frame) rows, not one million full
  frames.

[L2 slide 26: "Micro camera"]
  Image-only slide — a very small form-factor camera. Look up by the
  caption "Micro camera". Its significance is only that the physical size
  of the camera is a design constraint (L2 slide 3 slide "Mounting →
  limited space" on the checklist file).

2.14  3-D IMAGING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slides 27–32: "3D imaging" — six techniques]
  Each method uses a different physical cue for depth. Know the cue and
  the failure mode.

  LASER TRIANGULATION  (slide 27)
    - A laser LINE is projected onto the object.
    - A height profile is generated per slice by measuring the
      displacement of the laser line in the camera image.
    - The laser + camera SCAN across the object to build a 3-D volume.
    - Fails when: specular reflections, occlusions, transparent objects.

  STEREO VISION  (slide 28)
    - Two cameras mounted with different perspectives of the SAME object.
    - Calibration aligns pixel information between the two cameras.
    - Depth = disparity between matched pixels (triangulation).
    - Fails when: textureless surfaces (no correspondence), repetitive
      textures (false matches).

  PROJECTED LIGHT / SHEET OF LIGHT  (slide 29)
    - A KNOWN light pattern (stripes, dots, structured light) is projected.
    - Depth is calculated by how the pattern is DISTORTED by the object
      surface.
    - Fails when: strong ambient light or very dark surfaces.

  DEPTH FROM FOCUS  (slide 30)
    - Take multiple images at different focus settings; the focus setting
      at which a given pixel is sharpest indicates its depth.
    - Fails when: low-texture scenes, heavy computation.

  TIME OF FLIGHT (ToF)  (slide 31)
    - Light source synchronised with an image sensor.
    - Distance = c·t/2 where t = time between emitted pulse and reflected
      pulse arriving back.
    - Covers medium range (0.1 – 10 m typical).
    - Fails when: strong ambient IR, multi-path reflections.

  LIDAR  (slide 32)
    - Laser scanning of an area; measures reflections to build a 3-D
      profile / map.
    - Formula (verbatim on the slide):
          d  =  c · t  /  (2 · n)
      with c = 300 000 km/s, t = time, n = refractive index of medium
      (n = 1 in air).
    - Fails when: rain / snow / fog, reflective or absorptive targets at
      range.

  Numeric anchor (LIDAR): 1 ns round-trip time in air ⇒ d = 3·10^8 · 1·10^-9
  / 2 ≈ 0.15 m. Practical LIDARs time pulses to ~100 ps to resolve ~1.5 cm.

2.16  FRAME GRABBER / INTERFACE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 35: "Frame grabber"]
  Interface table (verbatim):

    Standard               Max length (m)    Max speed (MB/s)
    ---------------------  ----------------  ----------------
    USB 2.0                      5                  400
    USB 3.0                      5                 4800
    1394a (Firewire A)           4.5                400
    1394b (Firewire B)           4.5                800
    Future 1394c (Firewire C)    --                3200
    CameraLink                  10                 6120
    Gigabit Ethernet (GigE)    100                 1000
    Gigabit Ethernet (10GigE)  100                10000
    CoaxPress                  100                10000

  Named concepts on the same slide:
    Repeaters — hardware that restores signal integrity so a bus can be
    chained beyond its single-segment length.
    Overhead — the fraction of bus bandwidth consumed by protocol /
    handshaking; effective throughput is lower than the "max speed"
    column.

  Numeric anchor (bandwidth sizing): a 4 MPixel 8-bit colour image at 30
  fps carries 4·10^6 · 3 · 30 = 360 MB/s. That fits on USB 3.0 (4.8 GB/s)
  but saturates USB 2.0 (400 MB/s); it fits comfortably on CameraLink
  (6.1 GB/s) and GigE (1 GB/s); 10GigE is for 8K/high-fps cameras.

  The syllabus Q4-1b ("hardware trends") wants this table read as: bandwidth
  rising monotonically from USB 2.0 to CoaxPress/10GigE while cable length
  is traded (CameraLink 10 m at 6 GB/s vs GigE 100 m at 1 GB/s).

2.19  LENS SYSTEMS — pinhole, Gaussian, refraction
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 39: "Lens"]
  Image-only slide. Caption "Lens". Shows a real lens cross-section.

[L2 slide 40: "Lens system — the pinhole camera"]
  Variables:
    h  = object size
    h' = image size
    s  = object distance
    s' = image distance
    c  = optical centre distance (from pinhole to image)
    α  = half-angle subtended by the image

  Relationships shown on the slide:
    h' / c  =  2 · tan(α)
    h' / s' =  h / s   (equal because the same α subtends both the object
                        at distance s and the image at distance s')

  Interpretation: this is pure geometry. No lens, only a tiny hole. The
  pinhole model is used as the denominator of every distortion model in L5
  camera calibration.

[L2 slide 41: "Lens system" — c, h, h', s, s']
  Same as slide 40 but with the optical centre c marked and the two
  triangles (object-side, image-side) drawn explicitly. Look up by the
  caption "Lens system".

[L2 slide 42: "Lens system — Gaussian optics"]
  Same variable set, but F and F' mark the FOCAL POINTS and f, f' the
  focal distances. See next slide for the formulas.

[L2 slide 43: "Gaussian optics" formulas]
  Amplification (magnification):
        β  =  h' / h  =  s' / s
  Gaussian lens formula:
        1/s'  +  1/s  =  1/f'
  And the same tan α equality as the pinhole:
        h'/s'  =  h/s  =  tan α

  Interpretation: replace the pinhole by a lens and the geometry becomes
  Gaussian; the focal length f' now determines where the image plane must
  sit for a given object distance s. Every focal-length question on the
  exam uses these three relationships in some combination.

[L2 slide 44: "Lens — example" (Snell's law)]
  Refractive indices:
    Glass 1.5, Water 1.33, Air 1.
  Law of refraction (as written on the slide):
        n1 · sin θ2  =  n2 · sin θ1
  [note] Standard form is n1 · sin θ1 = n2 · sin θ2. The slide's
  labelling of θ1/θ2 is swapped; the numeric example below uses the
  standard form.

  Numeric example on the slide:
    1.33 · sin(45°)  =  1.5 · sin(θ2)   ⇒   θ2  =  38.8°
    1.5  · sin(38.8°) =  1   · sin(θ3)   ⇒   θ3  =  70.1°

  Interpretation: light bends toward the normal when going from water
  (n = 1.33) to glass (n = 1.5), and away from the normal going from glass
  back to air (n = 1). This is why a simple glass ball can focus; a
  multi-element lens corrects the spherical and chromatic aberrations that
  the single Snell-law bend cannot.

2.21  TELECENTRIC LENSES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slide 46: "Telecentric lenses"]
  Image-only slide, but the syllabus (§4.2) gives the definition:

  Telecentric lenses are used when the field of view is SMALLER THAN OR
  NEAR TO camera sensor size.
    - Magnification 0.01× up to several hundred ×.
    - Provide LOW DISTORTION and near-exact image reproduction.
    - Never used for large FOV because the lens would have to be as large
      as the object.
    - Specified in MAGNIFICATION (image size / sensor size).
    - Working distance is RIGIDLY FIXED by the lens maker.

  Selection rule: compute the required magnification, then pick the
  nearest standard magnification.

  Two lens classes (from §4.2):
    1. FOV ≫ sensor — fixed-focal or zoom lens, selection by focal
       length formula.
    2. FOV ≈ sensor — telecentric, selection by magnification.

2.23  ILLUMINATION — sources

[L2 slides 49–55: "Illumination" sources]

  narrow emission peak of a red LED — the reason LEDs give predictable
  colour response when paired with a filter.

  Numeric anchor (Xenon vs LED): Xenon strobing 200 flashes/s at 1–20 µs
  per flash = 0.02%–0.4% duty cycle. That produces an effective exposure
  equivalent to continuous light at ~30–800 W while consuming pulsed
  energy only during the flash — which is why strobes are chosen for
  high-speed capture (L2 slide 57 "High speed strobe light").

2.24  ILLUMINATION — geometry
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[L2 slides 56–58: "Illumination" geometries]
  Four canonical geometries:

    BACK LIGHTNING  (slide 56)
      - Silhouette against a bright background.
      - Uses: detecting presence / absence of holes and gaps; measuring
        objects; part placement / orientation.

    DIFFUSE (FULL BRIGHT FIELD) LIGHTNING  (slide 57)
      - Non-directional.
      - Produces MINIMUM SHADOWS.
      - For curved, specular surfaces: DOME diffuse light (surrounds the
        part) or ON-AXIS diffuse light (for flat samples).
      - Fluorescent is the simplest and most common diffuse source.
      - Requires close proximity to the sample to be effective.

    PARTIAL BRIGHT FIELD / DIRECTED LIGHTNING  (slide 58)
      - Directional, typically from a point source.
      - Good for contrast and topographic detail.
      - Ineffective on-axis with specular surfaces (generates "hotspot"
        reflection).

    DARK FIELD LIGHTNING  (slide 58)
      - Low-angle light.
      - Only light scattered from EDGES and SURFACE IMPERFECTIONS enters
        the camera; flat reflective regions send light away.
      - Uses: surface flatness and texture, surface reflectivity.

  Additional illumination slides:

4. SUMMARY (exam-ready, 12 bullets)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Machine vision = Illumination → Lens → Camera → Sensor → Computer.
   Four distances define the setup: DoF, working distance, resolution, FOV.
2. Camera internals: lens, image sensor, sensor driver, communication
   interface (Analog/USB/Ethernet/Camera Link/FireWire).
3. Sensor families: CCD (charge transported, high quality, power-hungry,
   blooming risk) vs CMOS (per-pixel amplifier, cheap, low power, rolling
   shutter typical, ROI windowing possible).
4. CCD variants: full frame (smears, ~100% fill factor) vs frame transfer
   (<500 µs shielded transfer, ~100% fill factor, expensive).
5. CMOS architecture: RAM-style row/column addressing; low fill factor →
   low SNR unless microlenses used.
6. Bayer filter: 2×2 RGGB pattern, single-channel per pixel, demosaicing
   reconstructs missing channels; colour moiré risk.
7. Shutters: global (all pixels simultaneous, no motion artefact) vs
   rolling (row-by-row, higher sensitivity, motion skew).
8. Noise: temporal (photon shot + thermal → reduce by averaging 1/sqrt(N))
   vs spatial (sensitivity + dark-signal variation → pattern noise, needs
   flat-field).
9. CCD wins only <4 photons/pixel (SNR graph crossover); CMOS wins
   elsewhere — hardware trend line.
10. Read-out modes: interlaced (odd/even fields, flicker, artefact) vs
    progressive (sequential, motion-safe). Acquisition modes: synchronous
    (< half frame speed), asynchronous (≤ frame speed), triggered
    asynchronous (≤ frame speed).
11. 3-D imaging: laser triangulation, stereo vision, projected/sheet of
    light, depth from focus, time of flight, LIDAR
    (d = c·t / (2·n)).
12. Lens formula trio: β = h'/h = s'/s;  1/s' + 1/s = 1/f';
    focal-length estimate f = (β · WD) / (1 + β) where β = sensor size /
    FOV. Snell: n1·sinθ1 = n2·sinθ2.
13. Illumination sources: sun, fluorescent, xenon (5500–12000 K, 1–20 µs
    flash), halogen, mercury, LED, HPS. Four geometries: back, diffuse
    (dome/on-axis), partial-bright-field (directional), dark-field.
14. Software: LabVIEW, HALCON, Matrox, OpenCV, ImageJ, MATLAB. This course
    uses MATLAB + IPT + CV System Toolbox + Statistics/ML Toolbox.

EXAMPLE A — focal length from FOV + sensor + working distance
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[Matches syllabus Q4-2 and sample-exam Q1 in style.]

Problem: image a 5-cm-wide object with a CCD whose sensor measures
8.8 × 6.6 mm at a working distance of 0.5 m.

  FOV        =  50 mm
  Sensor     =  6.6 mm       (the dimension that matches the 50 mm FOV
                              — the syllabus answer uses the vertical
                              dimension because the object is described as
                              "5 cm wide" and the sensor's matching axis is
                              6.6 mm; use the sensor dimension parallel to
                              the object dimension you are constraining.)
  WD         =  500 mm

  Magnification β  =  sensor_size / FOV
                   =  6.6 / 50
                   =  0.132
  Focal length f   =  β · WD / (1 + β)
                   =  0.132 · 500 / 1.132
                   =  66 / 1.132
                   =  58.30 mm

  Nearest standard focal lengths (from syllabus Q4-2): 50 or 75 mm.
  Decision: a 50 mm gives a LARGER field of view (lower resolution);
  a 75 mm gives a SMALLER FOV (higher resolution) but may not fit the
  full 5 cm object at 500 mm WD.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMPLE B — focal length from FOV + sensor + working distance
[Matches syllabus Q4-3.]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Problem: Mako G-125B/C, 1/3-inch sensor, FOV = 50 mm (horizontal),
WD = 200 mm.

  Sensor horizontal  =  4.8 mm  (1/3-inch standard)
  FOV                =  50 mm
  WD                 =  200 mm

  β  =  4.8 / 50     =  0.096
  f  =  0.096 · 200 / (1 + 0.096)
     =  19.2 / 1.096
     =  17.52 mm

  Nearest standard: 18 mm lens (bigger FOV, slightly lower resolution) or
  25 mm + extension ring (smaller FOV, higher resolution).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMPLE C — CCD resolution in line pairs / mm
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[Matches sample-exam Q1.]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Problem: CCD 4 × 9 mm, 512 × 1024 elements, image distance s' = 40 mm,
object distance s = 70 cm = 700 mm.

  Step 1: magnification (using the 4-mm axis)
        β  =  s' / s  =  40 / 700  =  0.05714
  Step 2: object height that maps to the 4-mm sensor axis
        h  =  h' / β  =  4 / 0.05714  =  70 mm  (= 0.07 m)
  Step 3: sensor element density along that axis
        512 elements / 4 mm  =  128 elements / mm at the sensor
        At the object plane the density is the same: 512 elements / 70 mm
        =  7.31 elements / mm
  Step 4: conversion from elements/mm to line-pairs/mm
        Line-pair = one bright + one dark stripe = 2 elements.
        Resolution  =  7.31 / 2  =  3.66 line-pairs / mm
        Rounded:  3.7 line-pairs / mm  ✓

  Numeric sanity: 1/3.7 ≈ 0.27 mm per line-pair at the object plane — the
  smallest reliably resolvable stripe pair is ~0.27 mm.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMPLE D — Snell's law
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[Matches L2 slide 44 example.]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Light in water (n = 1.33) hits glass (n = 1.5) at 45°.

  n1 · sin θ1  =  n2 · sin θ2
  1.33 · sin(45°)  =  1.5 · sin(θ2)
  1.33 · 0.7071    =  1.5 · sin(θ2)
  0.9404           =  1.5 · sin(θ2)
  sin(θ2)          =  0.6269
  θ2               =  38.8°          ✓

  Then from glass (1.5) to air (1.0) at 38.8°:
  1.5 · sin(38.8°)  =  1 · sin(θ3)
  1.5 · 0.6269      =  0.9404
  sin(θ3)           =  0.9404
  θ3                =  70.1°          ✓

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMPLE E — bandwidth sizing
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Problem: 4-megapixel colour camera at 30 fps, 8 bits per channel. Does
GigE suffice?

  Frame size  =  4 · 10^6 pixels × 3 channels × 1 byte = 12 MB / frame
  Frame rate  =  30 fps
  Data rate   =  12 MB × 30  =  360 MB/s  =  2880 Mbps  ≈ 2.88 Gbps

  GigE (1000 Mbps) → NOT sufficient.
  10GigE (10000 Mbps) → sufficient.
  CameraLink Base (~6.8 Gbps) → sufficient.

  This is why the L2 frame-grabber table matters: a modern camera can
  easily saturate GigE, forcing 10GigE / CoaxPress / CameraLink.

Q4-1  Based on this syllabus and the internet, describe:
      a) Pros and cons of CCD and CMOS based cameras.
      b) Hardware trends
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Official answer (verbatim summary — the PDF credits Mark Staarink, Gijs
de Vries, and Matthijs van Delft):

  "The main difference between CCD and CMOS sensors is how/when the
   electron charge (generated by the photo-electric effect) is converted
   to a voltage. CCD sensors buffer this voltage and output it as an
   analog signal. In CMOS sensors, the voltage gets converted to bits as
   soon as it's generated by a photo-sensitive cell. Note how CCD sensor
   cells only have to bother with the photo-electric effect, whereas in
   CMOS' sensor cells are required to do more than just that (like noise
   reduction and conversion to bits). This gives CMOS the advantage in
   speed, at the cost of uniformity (and thus image quality).

   As technology advanced however, it became possible to make CMOS
   sensors where the loss in image quality became negligible. In other
   fields, like energy efficiency, dynamic range and overall sensor
   reliability, CMOS has also managed to surpass CCD.

   Still, there are some inherent differences that still make CCD the
   preferred type for some applications. CCD sensors are still cheaper to
   design/manufacture when specific requirements are in play, rather than
   being able to settle for a generic, mass-produced CMOS solution. They
   are also more sensitive to light, and provide marginally better results
   when detecting light in the infrared spectrum.

   Technology continues to improve, good solutions are getting cheaper but
   requirements also become increasingly demanding. Not all older
   alternatives die out: some continue to see use, but they have to adapt
   to the increase in performance, quality.

   CMOS-sensors are cheaper, CMOS-sensors are more energy efficient,
   CMOS-sensors are less complex, CMOS-sensors have pixels that are
   controlled individually, CMOS-sensor have less interference compared
   to some CCD, CMOS-sensor often have a rolling shutter (better light
   sensitivity), CMOS-sensors have higher dynamic range, CMOS-sensors
   compute images faster and use windowing, CMOS-sensors are slightly
   better than CCD with UV lighting, CMOS-sensors are digital (no data
   loss when using analogue to digital converters), CMOS-sensors can be
   equipped with a global shutter, CMOS-sensors have higher framerate
   possibilities

   CMOS-sensors often have a rolling shutter (disturbance with moving
   objects), Production of smaller numbers of CMOS-sensors are more
   expensive than CCD sensors.

   CCD-sensors have a higher pixel density, CCD-sensors produce a better
   uniform image

   CCD-sensors can have blooming problems"

  [note] Part (b) "Hardware trends" is answered by the paragraph in the
  middle: "Technology continues to improve, good solutions are getting
  cheaper but requirements also become increasingly demanding. Not all
  older alternatives die out: some continue to see use, but they have to
  adapt." Read that as the trend statement.

Q4-2  It is required to image a 5-cm-wide object using a CCD camera whose
      sensor measures 8.8 × 6.6 mm at a distance of 0.5 m. What focal
      length lens should you choose?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Official answer (verbatim):

  "FOV             = 50 mm
   Sensor size     = 6.6 [mm]
   Working distance = 500 [mm]

   Magnification of an image acquired
       = (Sensor Size of the camera)/(Field of View)
       = 6.6 / 50 = 0.132 [mm]

   Focal Length
       = (Magnification)*(Working Distance)/(1+Magnification)
       = 0.132 * 500 / (1+0.132) = 58.30 [mm]

   Based on some desktop study, common focal lengths are:
       3.5, 4.5, 5, 6, 8, 12, 16, 17, 25, 28, 35, 50, 75 and 100 mm

   So depending on the priorities (FOV versus Resolution) the best choice
   might be a 50 or a 75 mm lens."

  [note] The PDF uses the 6.6-mm sensor dimension (vertical). This is the
  axis parallel to the 5-cm object's *height*; if "5 cm wide" refers to
  the horizontal, the 8.8-mm dimension should be used, giving β = 0.176
  and f ≈ 74.8 mm (i.e. nearest standard 75 mm). The PDF's 58.30 mm is the
  official answer, so write it that way on the exam; note the alternative
  in the working.

Q4-4  Explain shortly the following characteristics of illumination.
      a) Diffuse light
      b) Directed light
      c) Telecentric light
      d) Front light
      e) Back light
      f) Bright field
      g) Dark field
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Official answers (verbatim, one per sub-question):

  a) "Diffuse light is scattered in all directions in contrast to direct
      light. Think about the light during a cloudy day. In this case the
      direct light form the sun is scattered due to reflections in
      clouds."

  b) "In contrast to diffuse light, directed light is light in a single
      direction. Directed light is emitted from point sources."

  c) "In case of telecentric illumination, the light source emits only
      parallel light rays in a single direction."

  d) "If the light source is on the same side of the object as the
      camera, we speak of front light. This is also often referred to as
      incident light."

  e) "If the light source is on the opposite side of the object as the
      camera, we speak of back light."

  f) "If the light source is placed at an angle to the object so that
      most of the light is reflected to the camera, we speak of
      bright-field illumination."

  g) "If the light is placed in such a way that most of the light is
      reflected away from the camera, and only light of certain parts of
      the object is reflected to the camera, we speak of dark-field
      illumination."
