1. WHAT THE MODULE IS
================================================================

The module is an image processing / machine vision course built around
the classical digital image processing pipeline and ending in pattern
recognition. The course code in all documentation is EVD2; the module
in the minor is referred to as IPF.

Despite the minor title mentioning deep learning and machine learning,
the actual taught content is overwhelmingly CLASSICAL image processing
plus two classical classifiers (kNN and a single-layer perceptron).
Deep learning only appears as an OPTIONAL self-paced MATLAB Academy
course linked from the webpage (Deep Learning Onramp, Explore CNNs,
Tune Deep Learning Options). So there is a gap between the minor's
marketing title and the module's core examinable content. The exam is
about classical IP.

The single lecturer for everything is Roel Jansen (roel.jansen@han.nl),
who authored the Syllabus, the lesson decks, the answers, the scripts,
and the demos. The material is very much one person's course: consistent
style, consistent use of MATLAB, consistent image set.

The course is organized as: lectures (decks) -> Syllabus chapters with
numbered questions -> MATLAB exercises -> answer PDFs -> scripts ->
sample exam. Everything is aligned to Gonzalez & Woods, 4th edition
(2018) as the reference text, with Zhou et al. (2010) Part I/II as
secondary free reading. Note the 2026/2027 syllabus revision explicitly
REMOVED compulsory literature because the Zhou bookboon books are no
longer available — so the Syllabus itself plus the decks are now the
primary source.

2. LEARNING OUTCOMES (OFFICIAL)
================================================================

Ten stated intended learning outcomes:

 1. Know what digital image processing is; know frequency bands and the
    fundamental DIP steps.
 2. Know DIP fundamentals: human eye, light, EM spectrum; sensors for
    image acquisition; simple image formation model.
 3. Know physical properties of optical instruments and light, and how
    to apply them.
 4. Apply sampling and quantization; know basic pixel relations; apply
    the mathematical tools important to DIP.
 5. Know and apply intensity transformations (negative, log, gamma,
    piecewise linear).
 6. Know and apply histogram processing (equalization, matching/
    specification, local histogram processing, histogram statistics).
 7. Know and apply spatial filtering (smoothing and sharpening).
 8. Know and apply morphological operators (erosion, dilation,
    opening, closing).
 9. Know and apply segmentation operators (point, line, edge detection)
    and thresholding methods (global basic, Otsu optimum global).
10. Describe shape and boundaries of a segment using statistical
    moments.

Read carefully: outcomes 1-10 map almost one-to-one onto lessons 1-13.
Outcome 9 covers edge detection and thresholding. Pattern recognition
(lesson 14) is NOT in the numbered outcomes, yet it IS on the sample
exam indirectly (confusion matrix/precision-recall concepts are
referenced in the exam answer explanations). The morphological and
segmentation outcomes are the highest-numbered, i.e. the "later"
material that the exam weights.

4. LESSON-BY-LESSON CONTENT
================================================================

--- LESSON 1: INTRODUCTION ---
Defines the field. Applications: medical (ultrasound, X-ray, MRI, CT),
forensic, military, transportation, internet, industrial/robotics,
consumer electronics, research.
Machine vision basic steps: Image Acquisition -> Preprocessing ->
Segmentation -> Labeling -> Feature extraction -> Classification.
Note the "data intensity -> data abstraction" axis: each stage reduces
data and increases abstraction.
Image formats: binary, grayscale, indexed, RGB, HSI/HSV/HSL, L*a*b.
Human vision: cones (6-7 million, colour), rods (75-150 million, low
light), fovea 1.5x1.5 mm with ~150k elements/mm2, focal length 14-17 mm.
Optical illusions (Adelson checker shadow), Weber's law (~1-2%).
Metadata/Exif, bitplane slicing, 8-bit = 256 levels.
Digitization = sampling + quantization. Spatial resolution (dpi),
pixelation, jaggedness, moire. Aliasing and Nyquist: sample at >= 2x
highest frequency; undersampling -> aliasing, jaggies, moire;
wagon-wheel effect as temporal aliasing.
Pixel relationships: 4-, 8-, mixed(m)-adjacency; paths; connectivity;
components; regions; boundaries.

--- LESSON 2: HARDWARE AND SOFTWARE ---
The four hardware pillars: illumination, optical system (lens), camera
(sensor), computer.
Key distances: depth of field (focus range), working distance,
resolution (minimum feature size), field of view.
Camera internals: image sensor, sensor driver, communication hardware
interface (analog, USB, Ethernet, Camera Link, FireWire), external
interface.
Sensors: CCD vs CMOS.
  - CCD: charge transported across chip and read at one corner; high
    fill factor; high SNR; low noise; high power; expensive; blooming;
    full-frame CCD has smearing (needs shutter/strobe); frame-transfer
    CCD reduces smearing via <500 us transfer but is costly.
  - CMOS: per-pixel transistors, charge converted to bits locally;
    random pixel access (ROI, windowing); lower fill factor hence
    lower SNR; low power; cheap; high speed; higher dynamic range;
    rolling shutter typical (motion artefacts) though global shutter
    exists; microlenses used to raise fill factor.
  - Modern verdict: CMOS has largely caught up and surpassed CCD except
    for extreme low light (<4 photons/pixel) and some IR/UV cases.
Shutters: mechanical vs electronic; global (simultaneous sample/reset)
vs rolling (sequential, interleaved readout, better sensitivity).
Noise: photon/shot (Poisson), thermal, background (temporal — reduce
by averaging over time); sensitivity variation, dark signal variation
(spatial — manufacturing/pattern noise).
Readout modes: interlaced (odd/even fields, less flicker, moving
artefact) vs progressive (sequential rows).
Acquisition modes: synchronous free-running (process < half frame),
asynchronous free-running (interleaved, <= frame rate), triggered
asynchronous from async-reset camera (snapshot).
High-speed cameras: 25,000 fps at 1 MP; 1,000,000 fps at reduced res.
3D imaging: stereo vision, laser triangulation, projected light/sheet
of light, depth from focus, time of flight, LiDAR (d = c*t/(2n)).
Camera specs: pixel size, pixel depth (8/10/12 bit = 256/1024/4096),
gain and offset, spectral sensitivity, Bayer filter (mosaic + interp).
Frame grabber interfaces with bandwidth and max cable length table:
USB2 5m/400MB/s, USB3 5m/4800, FireWire A/B 4.5m/400-800,
CameraLink 10m/6120, GigE 100m/1000, 10GigE 100m/10000,
CoaxPress 100m/10000.
GenICam standard: GenApi, SFNC, GenTL; vendor list (Basler, Allied
Vision, Dalsa, JAI, Matrox, MVTec, NI, Pleora, Stemmer, Point Grey...).
Lens: pinhole model h'/s' = h/s = 2tan(alpha); Gaussian optics
1/s' + 1/s = 1/f'; magnification beta = h'/h = s'/s; refraction law
n1 sin(theta2) = n2 sin(theta1); depth of field; telecentric lenses.
Filters: coloured glass (cheap, wide cut-on, angle-independent) vs
coated interference (sharp cut-on/off, high transmission; longpass,
shortpass, bandpass, notch); colour filter example: green glass filter
raised green/red capsule contrast from 8.7% to 86.5%; polarizing
filter (polarizer on light + analyzer on lens) for specular surfaces.
Illumination: sources — fluorescent, quartz halogen/fibre, LED, metal
halide/mercury, xenon, high-pressure sodium. Selection trade-offs
(brightness, spectrum, stability, life, cost). Four cornerstones of
vision lighting: geometry, pattern/structure, wavelength, filters.
Acceptance criteria: maximize contrast on features of interest,
minimize elsewhere, be robust.
Techniques: back lighting (silhouette; holes/gaps/presence/measurement),
diffuse/full bright field (dome, on-axis; curved specular surfaces,
flat textured surfaces; non-directional, minimal shadow), partial
bright field / directional (most common, sunlight-like, good for
topography, hotspot on specular), dark field (low-angle; surface
flatness/texture/reflectivity; scratches). Application field map:
surface flatness/texture vs surface reflectivity (matte/mirror/mixed).
Software: LabVIEW, HALCON, Matrox, OpenCV, ImageJ, MATLAB.

--- LESSON 3: MATLAB BASICS ---
Why MATLAB: quick to learn, integrated, hardware interface, platform
independent, scientific standard, rapid development. Toolboxes relevant:
Image Acquisition, Computer Vision System, Image Processing, Vision HDL,
Statistics, Machine Learning.
Environment: current directory, workspace, command window, editor,
preview. Scripts vs functions vs anonymous functions.
Data classes: double, single, uint8/16/32, int8/16/32 — with ranges.
The critical rule: uint8 saturates/truncates, double preserves negatives
and precision. This rule recurs in Laplacian filtering, arithmetic,
division.
Matrix ops vs elementwise ops: ', inv, det, flipud, fliplr, rot90,
reshape, trace. Standard arrays: zeros, ones, true, false, eye, magic,
rand, randn.
Relational/logical operators and functions: < <= > >= == ~= & | ~ xor
all any.
Built-in constants: ans, eps, realmin, realmax, flintmax, NaN, Inf, pi,
i/j, clock, date.
Arithmetic operators table including .*, ./, .\, .^ and the function
equivalents (plus, times, rdivide, mpower...).
Script structure: definition line, H1 line, help text, body.
Control statements: for, while, if, elseif, switch-case. Timing: tic/
toc, profile on/viewer, vectorization.
IPT essentials: class conversions (im2double, im2uint8, im2uint16,
im2single, im2int16, ind2gray, gray2ind, rgb2gray, rgb2ind, ind2rgb);
display (image, imagesc, imshow, imtool); arithmetic (imadd,
imsubtract, immultiply, imdivide, imabsdiff, imcomplement, imlincomb).
Apps: Camera Calibrator, Color Thresholder, Image Acquisition, Image
Batch Processor, Image Region Analyzer, Image Segmenter, Image Viewer,
Stereo Image Calibrator, Training Image Labeler, OCR Trainer.
Style guidelines: naming, statements, layout/comments, files.
Demonstrations: pendulum, HDL pixel streaming, FPGA implementation via
Vision HDL Toolbox. Forward look: image category classification, OCR,
optical flow/tracking, 3D vision/Kinect, point cloud registration,
C code generation, HDL generation.
Key message: MATLAB is the vehicle; you must be fluent before the
image content lands.

--- LESSON 4: MATHEMATICAL TOOLS / ARITHMETIC AND LOGIC ---
Array (outer) product vs matrix (inner/dot) product — the distinction
that recurs throughout. Matrix product used for rotation/affine mapping.
Convolution and correlation:
  1D: (A*B)(x) = sum_j A(j) B(x-j)
  2D: g(x,y) = sum_k sum_j h(j,k) f(x-j, y-k) over half-widths m2, n2.
  Worked example A={0,1,2,3,2,1,0}, B={1,3,-1} mirrored -> 1,5,8,...
  Correlation = convolution without the 180-degree flip of the mask.
  For symmetric masks the difference is irrelevant.
Properties: commutative, associative, distributive.
Separable kernels: s = [1;2;1] * [-1 0 1] — compute cost drops from
PQ to P+Q per pixel; 9x9 kernel -> 4.5x theoretical speed-up.
Gaussian blur: h(x,y) = exp(-(x^2+y^2)/(2 sigma^2)); separable;
Fourier transform of Gaussian is Gaussian; convolution of two
Gaussians is a Gaussian; rotationally symmetric so no directional bias.
Linear filtering requirements: superposition R(f+g)=R(f)+R(g) and
scaling R(kf)=kR(f).
Arithmetic ops: addition (blend, offset, noise), with overflow handling
by truncation or normalization (the imadd example with uint8 vs uint16
intermediate and rescaling to [0,255]); subtraction (angiography, flat
field correction for sensor dust/uneven illumination); multiplication/
division (brighten/darken, dynamic scaling, background division for
gradient-illuminated text).
Functions: imadd, imsubtract, imabsdiff, imcomplement, immultiply,
imdivide.
Logic ops: bitand, bitor, bitxor, bitcmp (unsigned ints only) vs
imcomplement (binary/gray/RGB). Examples: masking, difference finding,
ROI processing with roipoly + uint8 conversion + bitand/bitor/bitcmp.
Applications: simultaneous visible + NIR capture; NDVI =
(nir - red)/(nir + red).

--- LESSON 5: GEOMETRIC OPERATIONS ---
Modify geometry: reposition pixels in a constrained way; used for
distortion correction, special effects, image registration.
Two components: mapping function (spatial transformation equations)
and interpolation method.
Affine mapping: [x'; y'; 1] = [a0 a1 a2; b0 b1 b2; 0 0 1][x; y; 1].
Table of coefficients: identity, scaling [sx,sy], counterclockwise
rotation by theta [cos sin 0; -sin cos 0], shear [shx], shear [shy],
translation [1 0 tx; 0 1 ty].
Interpolation: nearest neighbour (fastest, blocky), bilinear (2x2
weighted average, smoother), bicubic (4x4, sharpest, best compromise),
adaptive vs non-adaptive.
MATLAB: imresize, imrotate, imcrop, flipud, fliplr, maketform/
imtransform, affine2d + imwarp.
Aliasing: downsampling without prefiltering causes aliasing; prefilter
with a low pass (Gaussian) before downsampling.
Image registration: cpselect, cpcorr to refine, cp2tform (e.g.
nonreflective similarity), imwarp, overlay with AlphaData.
Calibration: correct lens distortion, measure in world units, locate
camera in 3D scene. World coordinates -> camera rigid coordinates
(extrinsic) -> pixel projective coordinates (intrinsic). Intrinsic:
focal length, lens distortion, sensor format, principal point.
Extrinsic: position and orientation.

--- LESSON 6: INTENSITY TRANSFORMATIONS AND HISTOGRAMS ---
Enhancement goals: subjective quality for humans; suitability for
further analysis. Goal-specific, interactive, subjective. Feedback is
application-specific.
Point transform: g(x,y) = T[f(x,y)].
Linear: s = c*r + b. Identity. Negative (imcomplement).
Auto-contrast: s = (L-1)(r - rmin)/(rmax - rmin) — the "scale to []"
operation.
Power law / gamma: s = c * r^gamma. gamma < 1 brightens shadows,
gamma > 1 darkens. Gamma correction.
Log: s = c*log(1+r). Compresses dynamic range, reveals hidden detail
(radio image example: range 224 -> 249 after log).
Piecewise linear: gray level slicing (enhance a band, suppress rest).
LUTs: for uint8, a 1x256 vector; intlut(A, LUT). Performance: LUT vs
direct computation (0.1655 s vs 0.0334 s in the syllabus example) —
roughly 5x.
Interactive: glsdemo, imcontrast.
Histogram: h(k) = n_k = card{(x,y) | f(x,y)=k}; normalized
p(rk) = n_k / n. Carries min/mean/max, dark/bright dominance; says
NOTHING about spatial distribution; cannot alone judge noise.
Equalization: histeq; flattens histogram; transformation function is
the normalized CDF; good for low contrast, BAD for bimodal images
(the "eight" coins-like example loses quality because two clusters are
widely spaced).
Matching/specification: histeq(I, hgram) — supply desired histogram
shape; interactive via ihmdemo.
Stretching: s = (L-1)(r-rmin)/(rmax-rmin).
Shrinking: maps to a narrower range [smin, smax].
Local equalization: adapthisteq, CLAHE, ClipLimit (default 0.01,
higher = more contrast), tiles (default 8x8 tiles, not a tile size).

--- LESSON 7: NEIGHBORHOOD PROCESSING ---
Reference point + neighborhood operation + write to output + repeat.
Linear filters = sum of products with mask coefficients; nonlinear =
ranking/sorting then select (median, min, max, midpoint, alpha-trimmed).
Mask/kernel/window terminology.
Edge handling: ignore, pad with zeros, mirror.
Low pass filters: attenuate high frequencies, blur and denoise.
Mean/box filter (1/9 uniform); mask size controls blur amount
(3x3 vs 9x9 shown); variations: weighted, directional, selective,
outlier removal.
Gaussian blur: non-uniform kernel sampled from 2D Gaussian.
Median filter: rank filter; excellent for salt & pepper, poor for
Gaussian (no extreme values to reject).
High pass: emphasize fine detail, points, lines, edges. Laplacian
(second derivative, isotropic, rotation invariant); produces double
edges and is noise sensitive; take absolute value or keep positives;
composite Laplacian mask (center 5) does subtract-in-one-step.
Directional difference filters / emboss masks.
Unsharp masking: original minus blurred -> sharpening image; three
implementations (blur + histogram shrink + subtract; subtract + add
back; single convolution mask via fspecial('unsharp')). High-boost
filtering: mask with A+4 centre; A=1 equals composite Laplacian; as A
grows the result approaches image * constant (A~2.7 in the answers).
Combining methods: no universal recipe; trial and error; sequence
matters.

--- LESSON 8: FREQUENCY DOMAIN FILTERING ---
Fourier transform: DC term = average brightness; sine patterns map to
single frequency points; multiple patterns combine; vertical/horizontal/
diagonal orientations map to oriented frequency pairs.
Pipeline: f(x,y) -> FT F(u,v) -> multiply by H(u,v) -> G(u,v) ->
inverse FT -> g(x,y). Convolution theorem: convolution in space =
multiplication in frequency.
Two ways to build filters: freqz2 from a spatial mask, or generate
directly in frequency domain with meshgrid + distance matrix.
Distance matrix: dist = sqrt(U.^2 + V.^2) with signed frequency
coordinates; fftshift for display; 3D mesh shows the cone/bowl shape.
Ideal LPF: H=1 if D<=D0 else 0; sharp cutoff -> ringing; smaller
radius = more blur, more ringing visible.
Gaussian LPF: H = exp(-D^2/(2 sigma^2)); smooth, no ringing, most
natural-looking.
Butterworth LPF: H = 1/(1 + (D/D0)^(2n)); n controls transition
sharpness; as n grows it approaches the ideal; advantage = tunable
trade-off between the two extremes.
High pass: ideal (1 outside, 0 inside), Gaussian (1 - exp(...)),
Butterworth (1/(1 + (D0/D)^(2n))). High-frequency emphasis filtering
HFE: Hd = a + b*H so you don't lose the DC/brightness.
Effects: increasing cutoff -> darker image until almost black (only
edges remain).
FT properties: separability (two 1D passes), rotation (rotate image ->
rotate spectrum).

--- LESSON 9: IMAGE RESTORATION ---
Degradation/restoration model. Noise models: Gaussian (amplifier
noise), impulse/salt-and-pepper (faulty switches), uniform, Rayleigh
(range imaging, MRI), gamma/Erlang (radar/LiDAR), exponential (laser
imaging).
Central limit theorem background, variance derivations, discrete
uniform distribution (mean (b+a)/2, variance ((b-a+1)^2-1)/12).
Noise effects on image and histogram: Gaussian broadens; salt & pepper
creates two spikes at 0 and 255; uniform flattens; Rayleigh, gamma,
exponential have characteristic skewed shapes.
Noise estimation.
Spatial noise reduction:
  - Arithmetic mean: best for Gaussian/uniform/Erlang, blurs
  - Geometric mean: retains detail better, Gaussian
  - Harmonic mean: good for Gaussian and salt, FAILS on pepper
    (1/0 -> Inf -> result 0)
  - Contraharmonic: Q negative for salt, positive for pepper; wrong
    sign amplifies the noise; cannot handle both simultaneously
  - Median: best all-round rank filter for salt & pepper
  - Min (removes salt), Max (removes pepper), Midpoint (Gaussian +
    uniform), Alpha-trimmed mean (mixed noise; D=0 -> arithmetic mean,
    D=(mn-1)/2 -> median)
Frequency domain: periodic noise from electrical/electromechanical
interference; bandreject, bandpass, notch (ideal, Butterworth,
Gaussian); notch pass = 1 - notch reject.
Deblurring: inverse filtering 1/H(u,v) — division-by-zero,
0/0 indeterminacy, noise amplification; fixes: Butterworth lowpass on
the division, or constrained division with threshold T.
Wiener filter: minimum mean-square error, H*(u,v) / (|H|^2 + K);
trade-off between restoration and noise suppression.
MATLAB: deconvwnr, deconvreg, deconvlucy, deconvblind.

--- LESSON 10: MORPHOLOGICAL IMAGE PROCESSING ---
Set theory refresher: Z, N, R; complement, intersection, union,
difference A-B = A intersect Bc, translation Ax, reflection B-hat.
Logical equivalents: intersection ~ AND, complement ~ NOT,
union ~ OR, difference ~ A AND (NOT B).
Definition: extract geometric/topological information from an unknown
set using a well-defined structuring element (SE). Jean Serra, 1980s.
SE design (shape and size) is crucial. strel shapes: arbitrary,
diamond, disk, line, octagon, pair, periodicline, rectangle, square;
flat vs nonflat (offsetstrel).
Fit vs hit.
Dilation: A(+)B = {z | (B-hat)_z intersect A != 0}; grows/thickens;
output = max in neighborhood; direction/size controlled by SE.
Erosion: A(-)B; shrinks/thins; output = min in neighborhood; SE must
fit.
Duality: (A - B)^c = A^c (+) B-hat ; A (+) B = (A^c - B-hat)^c.
Decomposition of large SEs into smaller ones.
Opening = erosion then dilation: removes small objects, breaks thin
connections, preserves size of remaining objects. imopen.
Closing = dilation then erosion: fills small holes, fuses breaks,
closes gaps, without changing object size as dilation would. imclose.
Hit-or-miss: two SEs (B1 hit, B2 miss), no shared elements; interval
encoding with 1/-1/0; bwhitmiss.
Boundary extraction: internal A - (A - B); external (A (+) B) - A;
morphological gradient combines both; bwperim with 4- or 8-connectivity.
Region filling: imfill; interactive with imfill(I).
Connected components: iterative Xk = (X(k-1) (+) B) intersect A;
bwlabel, bwselect, label2rgb. Each gray shade = one object.
Thinning (bwmorph 'thin'), thickening ('thicken' — stops at thickness
1), skeletonization ('skel', Inf — comparable to infinite thinning),
spur, bridge, clean, diag, endpoints, branchpoints, majority, shrink,
remove, fill, tophat, bothat, hbreak.
Grayscale morphology: dilation = max(f(x+s,y+t) [+bN]), erosion = min
(...); opening and closing as above; top-hat = f - (f o b); bottom-hat
= (f . b) - f; used for uneven illumination correction (the
color-plane -> erosion -> dilation -> threshold -> B-D pipeline).

--- LESSON 11: POINT, LINE, EDGE DETECTION ---
Point detection: Laplacian-like mask [-1 -1 -1; -1 8 -1; -1 -1 -1];
threshold the absolute response.
Line detection: directional masks.
Edge definition: boundary between regions with distinct characteristics.
Ideal step vs ramp model; gray level profile.
First derivative -> magnitude detects edge presence; second derivative
-> sign tells dark/bright side, zero crossing locates thick edge centre.
Noise sensitivity demonstrated with sigma 0.001 vs 0.05.
Pipeline: noise reduction -> convolution in two perpendicular
directions -> magnitude sqrt(d1^2+d2^2) -> direction arctan(d2/d1).
Operators:
  Roberts: 2x2 diagonal, poor on noise
  Prewitt: 3x3, [-1 0 1] rows and transpose
  Sobel: 3x3 with weights 1,2,1 — better on noiseless, similar on noisy
  Sharr: weighted 3,10,3
  Kirsch: 8 compass masks, max over all 8
  Robinson: 8 compass masks, similar
  Laplacian: second derivative; double edges; very noise sensitive;
    zero-cross option in edge
  LoG: Gaussian smoothing then Laplacian; "Mexican hat"; sigma controls
    scale; larger sigma -> fewer edges
  Canny: (1) Gaussian smoothing, (2) gradient magnitude+direction,
    (3) non-maximal suppression, (4) hysteresis thresholding with
    Tlow/Thigh, (5) edge linking of weak 8-connected to strong
Threshold t returned by edge(); higher threshold -> only strong edges
remain -> lower sensitivity.
Edge linking: edges are fragmented; Hough transform maps (x,y) to
(rho, theta) accumulator; peaks -> lines; houghpeaks, houghlines.
Circle detection: imfindcircles.
MATLAB: edge(I,'sobel'|'prewitt'|'roberts'|'log'|'canny'|'zerocross').

--- LESSON 12: IMAGE SEGMENTATION ---
Segmentation: partition image into non-overlapping regions whose union
is the whole image; regions should correspond to objects and background.
Based on discontinuity, similarity, or both. Still an open problem.
Easy vs hard images: occlusion, uneven illumination, shadows.
Three-category taxonomy used in the course:
  Intensity-based (non-contextual): pixel distributions/histograms,
    e.g. thresholding
  Region-based (contextual): adjacency and connectivity, e.g. region
    growing and split-and-merge
  Other: texture, edges, motion
Thresholding: g(x,y) = 1 if f(x,y) > T else 0; converts many gray
levels into two.
Global thresholding: one T for the whole image.
Otsu (1979): maximize between-class variance; graythresh; example gave
T = 0.4941.
Illumination and noise break global thresholding: even easy images get
hard. imnoise with Gaussian mean 0, variance 0.01/0.02.
Local/adaptive thresholding: block processing; block too small = costly,
too large = no better than global. Algorithms: Niblack, Sauvola
(normalized windowed std dev), modified Sauvola (windowed mean
deviation, cheaper). MATLAB: imbinarize(I,'adaptive',T), adaptthresh,
blockproc.
Region-based: pixel value alone insufficient; add connectivity.
Conditions on Ri: union = image, disjoint, connected, each satisfies
predicate Q.
Region growing algorithm:
  (a) find connected components of seeds S, erode each to one pixel
  (b) form fQ (1 where predicate Q holds)
  (c) append to each seed all 1-valued 8-connected pixels
  (d) label each connected component as a distinct region
Predicates/homogeneity criteria: pure uniformity; local mean vs global
mean; local std dev vs global mean; variance (percentage within 2 sigma);
texture (four quadrants comparable).
Key factors: similarity criterion, seed selection (interactive or
cluster-based), stopping rule.
Predicate form: TRUE if |f(x,y) - mu_PR_i| <= Delta.
Limitations: unstable between 4- and 8-connectivity; sensitive to the
predicate; insufficient seeds leave pixels unassigned; redundant seeds
force artificial splits.
Split and merge: start with whole image, split into quadrants until
each region homogeneous (quadtree, each node 4 children, leaves =
regions), then merge adjacent regions satisfying the union predicate.
Algorithm: define P, compute P(Ri), split quadrants where false, repeat,
merge adjacent where P(Rj union Rk) true, repeat.
Watershed: regions = catchment basins, boundaries = ridge lines;
watershed() function; build from distance transform.
Distance transform: distance from every pixel to nearest nonzero pixel.
City-block (Manhattan) distance D = sum |xki - xkj|. bwdist(a,'cityblock').
Active contours (snakes) mentioned.
Colour-based segmentation: rgb2ind(I, n); partition colour space with
thresholds.
MATLAB: imopen/imclose, watershed, graythresh, im2bw, imbinarize.

--- LESSON 13: FEATURE EXTRACTION ---
Feature extraction: detection and representation of features of
interest for further processing; transition from pictorial to
non-pictorial (alphanumeric/quantitative) data. Assumes segmentation
has already happened. Goal: convert segmented objects into
representations that describe their main features/attributes.
Feature vector: n x 1 array, symbolic and/or numerical; compact
representation; feature space = n-dimensional hyperspace; visualization
only for n < 4; distance in feature space relates to similarity.
Invariance and robustness: RST (rotation, scaling, translation)
invariance; application dependent.
Binary object features (binary object = connected region):
  Area Ai = sum sum Oi(x,y)
  Centroid xbar = (1/Ai) sum sum x Oi ; ybar similarly
  Moments m_pq = sum sum x^p y^q f(x,y)
  Central moments mu_pq = sum sum (x-xbar)^p (y-ybar)^q f(x,y)
    — translation invariant
  Normalized central moments n_pq = mu_pq / mu00^gamma,
    gamma = (p+q)/2 + 1 for p+q > 1 — scale invariant
  Axis of least second moment: tan 2 theta = 2*mu11/(mu20 - mu02)
  Projections h(x), v(y)
  Euler number E = C - H (components minus holes)
  Perimeter (count boundary pixels; bwperim)
  Thinness ratio T = 4*pi*Area / Perimeter^2 — roundness; approaches 1
    for a circle; inverse = irregularity/compactness ratio
  Eccentricity = ratio of major to minor axis (A/B)
  Aspect ratio = (xmax-xmin+1)/(ymax-ymin+1)
  Elongatedness a/b
  Hu's seven rotation/scale/translation invariant moments (1962)
MATLAB regionprops: Area, BoundingBox, Centroid, ConvexHull,
Eccentricity, EquivDiameter, EulerNumber, Extent, FilledArea,
FilledImage, MajorAxisLength, MinorAxisLength, Orientation, Perimeter,
PixelIdxList, PixelList, Solidity.
Boundary descriptors (contour-based): bug tracing / Moore boundary
following; bwtraceboundary(BW,[r c],'W',8,Inf,'counterclockwise');
bwboundaries.
Chain code: directions right 0, down 1, left 2, up 3 (crack code);
Freeman code: 8 directions.
Signatures: 1D boundary representation, distance r vs angle theta from
centroid; rotation and translation invariant, NOT scale invariant;
cart2pol in the example.
Fourier descriptors: treat (xk, yk) as complex xk + j yk; DFT of the
list is the descriptor; inverse DFT restores; few coefficients suffice
(100%, 50%, 25%, 2.5%, 1% reconstruction demonstration).
Histogram-based features: mean, standard deviation, skew, energy,
entropy — RST invariant.
Texture: no universal definition; smoothness/roughness, coarseness,
regularity. Roughness R = 1 - 1/(1+sigma^2).
GLCM (gray-level co-occurrence matrix): counts pixel pairs with
intensities zi, zj at displacement d = (dx,dy); normalize by total;
features: max probability, correlation, contrast, uniformity (energy),
homogeneity, entropy.
Summary: feature vector, invariance, binary features, translation
invariant moments, boundary descriptors, chain/Freeman code,
histogram features, texture features.

--- LESSON 14: PATTERN RECOGNITION ---
Goal: assign a class to each image/object based on a numerical
representation suitable for the problem.
Statistical vs structural/syntactic classifiers.
Unsupervised (no labels, find clusters) vs supervised (labels known).
Recap features: thinness ratio T = 4 pi Ai / Pi^2 (roundness), and
eccentricity = major/minor axis ratio.
Design and implementation steps:
  1. Define problem and number of classes
  2. Extract suitable features
  3. Select classification method
  4. Select dataset
  5. Select training subset and train
  6. Test
  7. Refine and improve
Pattern = arrangement of descriptors/features encoded as feature
vectors, strings, or trees. Class = set of patterns sharing common
properties. Ideal class: high intra-class similarity, high inter-class
difference (intracluster distances minimized, intercluster maximized).
Data preprocessing: noise/outlier removal; normalization (unit vector,
standard normal density (f-mu)/sigma, min-max, softmax); missing data.
Training vs test sets: training typically <= 20%, representative;
separate test set to avoid bias in reported success rates.
Worked example: X-ray detection of longhorn-beetle infested trees
(Jansen & Hemming 2011).
Classification measures:
  TP = correctly identified
  FP = incorrectly identified
  TN = correctly rejected
  FN = incorrectly rejected
  Sensitivity (recall) = TP / (TP + FN)
  Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + TN + FN)
  Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
Worked numbers: TP 5, TN 625, FP 300, FN 1, total 929.
Human+machine combination example: 100 artificial drilled holes;
machine vision alone 61% accuracy, MV+observer1 82%, MV+observer2 83%;
sensitivity 56/50/50%.
ROC curve: hit rate (TP rate) vs false alarm rate (FP rate); ideal
knee at top-left.
Confusion matrix: K x K; row i column j = number of times true class i
was labelled j; diagonal = successes; perfect classifier has all
off-diagonal zero. Example 4x4:
  w1: 97 0 2 1
  w2: 0 89 10 1
  w3: 0 0 100 0
  w4: 0 3 5 92
  Overall error = (3+11+0+8)/(4*100) = 5.5% -> success 94.5%.
Precision = TP/(TP+FP) (exactness); recall = TP/(TP+FN) (completeness);
P-R graph; F1 = 2 * (precision * recall)/(precision + recall).
Distance and similarity measures: Euclidean, vector inner product,
Tanimoto metric.
Statistical classification: project feature vectors into feature space;
same-class objects cluster; classifier finds a discrimination curve or
hyper-surface.
Three techniques listed: minimum distance classifier, kNN, Bayesian
(next year).
Minimum distance classifier: d_j(x) = |x - m_j|, m_j = class mean
vector; problems — multimodal classes (samples in disjoint clusters)
and irregular shapes.
k-Nearest Neighbours:
  Classify by majority of k closest examples.
  "Tell me who your neighbors are and I will tell you who you are."
  k=3 example: 2 apples 1 banana -> apple.
  Multiple classes easy (k=5, 3 bananas 1 apple 1 cucumber -> banana).
  Choosing k: theoretically larger k better with infinite samples, but
    all k must be close; rule of thumb k ~ sqrt(n); k=1 often used for
    efficiency but noise sensitive; too large k destroys locality;
    cross-validation to choose k.
  Distance: Euclidean treats all features equally; discriminative
    features can be swamped (extreme example: feature 1 gives class
    1 or 2, feature 2 is noise 100-150; [1 100] misclassified as
    class 2) — the denser the samples the less the problem, but samples
    are rarely dense enough.
  Feature normalization: linear scale to [0,1]
    fnew = (fold - fmin)/(fmax - fmin); or zero mean unit variance
    fnew = (fold - mu)/sigma; MATLAB matrix form with repmat/mean/std.
  MATLAB: fitcknn, knn.NumNeighbors, knn.Distance, knn.predict;
    confusionmat for evaluation; gscatter for feature plots.
  Advantages: any distribution, non-linear boundaries, simple,
    intuitive, good with enough samples.
  Disadvantages: choosing k, expensive test stage (no training stage —
    all work at test time, opposite of what is usually wanted), needs
    many samples.
Neural network classification: single-layer perceptron; inputs x1..xn,
weights w1..wn, bias, output; sigmoid 1/(1+exp(-y)) mapping to [0,1];
delta rule weight update w = w + coeff*input*delta; 100 iterations;
comparison plot of predictions; results similar to kNN.
Worked apple/banana dataset: 13 training samples, features thinness
ratio and eccentricity, test samples classified, confusion matrix with
5/1 and 0/4.

7. THE SCRIPTS
================================================================

c_harmonic.m — contraharmonic mean: y = sum(x.^(r+1))/sum(x.^r).
Used with nlfilter and an r parameter (negative for salt, positive for
pepper). This is the "filter without a convolution mask" pattern.

distmatrix.m — generates M-by-N matrix of distances to the centre.
Wraps u and v so frequencies above M/2 and N/2 become negative
(i.e. builds signed frequency coordinates), then meshgrid, then
sqrt(U.^2 + V.^2). This is the backbone of every frequency-domain
filter in the course: build dist, then write H as a function of dist.

raise_to_power.m — minimal function example used to teach function
structure (definition line, H1 line, help text, body).

script3_3_1.m — script vs function, comment line, comment before code
on the same line.

script3_3_2.m — cell mode demo: %% cells, gamma, imadjust(I,
stretchlim(I), [], gamma), four-panel original/adjusted image +
histogram. Used to teach "increment and run" on the gamma value.

fddemo.m — large GUI demo. Loads eight.tif by default (or a user
image), converts colour to monochrome. Displays original image,
original DFT (log(1+abs(fftshift))), filter profile, filtered image,
filtered DFT. Six filters: ideal/gaussian/butterworth low and high
pass. Ideal and Butterworth use a draggable magenta circle for cutoff;
Gaussian uses a standard deviation text box; Butterworth also has an
order text box; high-pass filters have a high-frequency emphasis
checkbox (a=1, b=1). Default cutoffs 40, default std 30, default order
2. The generate_filter subfunction is effectively the reference
implementation of every frequency filter equation in the course. The
Update subfunction does new_dft = dft .* filter; new_img =
real(ifft2(new_dft)). This is the single most useful script to read
before the exam.

glsdemo.m — interactive gray-level slicing. Loads circuit.tif by
default. Plot of transformation function 0..255 vs 0..255; click to
add a point, drag to move, click on a point to delete. Points are
linearly interpolated with interp1; result clamped to [0,255];
new_img = uint8(yi(img+1)) — note the +1 index offset that appears as
question 11.1. Has a "Continuous Update" checkbox.

ihmdemo.m — interactive histogram matching. Loads pout.tif by default.
You draw the desired histogram shape in normalized [0,1] space; the
interpolation method is selectable (spline/linear/nearest); the update
subfunction calls histeq(img, yi) and re-plots the matched histogram.
The x-axis is 0..1 not 0..255, which is why the answers about
"continuous update" and "interpolation method" were marked as
work-in-progress.

Reading the scripts together gives you the course's implementation
idiom: build a matrix or a vector, apply it, display with imshow(...,
[]) when values fall outside [0,255] or [0,1], and use fftshift when
displaying anything centred.

exactly those skill areas: FOV, working distance, smallest detail,
resolution, contrast, illumination geometry. If the exam asks a
"which illumination for this situation" question, the checklist's
vocabulary is the vocabulary to answer with.

10. THE SAMPLE EXAM
================================================================

Document: SampleExam EVD2_2025_2026_blok_1_including_answers_eng.
90 minutes. Open questions and multiple choice. Graphical calculator
permitted. Written or digital (choice). Academy AEA, period P1.
Revision note: 6/10/2025 revision table added; cover replaced;
"Question 4, bitwise operations explicitly mentioned" — meaning they
went out of their way to make the bitwise AND/OR/XOR requirement
explicit, so expect it.

Structure and content of the seven questions:

Q1 (calculation). CCD chip 4 x 9 mm, 512 x 1024 elements, image
distance 40 mm, focused on a flat area 70 cm away. Resolution in line
pairs/mm?
  Worked: 4/40 = h/700 -> h = 70 mm; 512 elements per line over 70 mm
  = 7.3 elements/mm; divide by 2 for line pairs = 3.7 lp/mm.
  Teaching point: this fuses the lens formula (h'/s' = h/s) with
  sampling (elements per unit object length) and the line-pair
  definition. This is the single most likely exam calculation type.

Q2 (MCQ). Illumination technique most suitable for diffuse
illumination of a relatively large area. Options: LED, fluorescence
(fluorescent tube), xenon, bulb. Correct: fluorescence. Teaching
point: fluorescent is the simplest and most common way to get diffuse
illumination over large areas; LEDs are not yet as cost-effective for
large areas.

Q3 (MCQ). Identify the illumination type in a figure. Options:
diffuse, direct, dome, telecentric. This tests recognition of
illumination geometry from a drawing — exactly the lesson 2 /
syllabus Q4-5 skill (the four-option A/B/C/D figure exercise).

Q4 (calculation). Two 3x3 images given; compute X AND Y, X OR Y,
X XOR Y using bitwise operations. The answer given shows all three
results equal for the specific operands (because the bit patterns
happen to give that), with the explanation that bitwise AND darkens
(lower values have fewer 1 bits), OR brightens, XOR highlights
differences. Teaching point: you must be able to do bitwise arithmetic
on uint8 values by hand, and explain the practical interpretation.

Q5 (conceptual + code reading). Sample MATLAB:
  A = eye(80,80); A(26:2:54,:)=1; B = imcomplement(A);
  C = imresize(B,0.5,'nearest','Antialiasing',false);
Question: is the objective (nearest-neighbour downsampling to 40x40)
attained? Answer: no — the sampling frequency is too low; it should be
at least 2x the highest frequency to recover (Nyquist). The montage
figure shows aliasing artefacts. Teaching point: connecting code to
the aliasing/Nyquist theory from lesson 1 and syllabus Q3.

Q6 (calculation). 4x4 image, V = {0,1}, compute shortest 4-, 8-, and
m-paths between p and q; explain if a path does not exist. Answer:
4-path does not exist; shortest 8-path length 4; shortest m-path
length 5. Teaching point: pure lesson 1 pixel-relationship material,
tested as a small hand computation. Note: this exact question also
appears in the lesson 1 deck as a worked example, so it is a very
likely repeat.

Q7 (multi-part, the big one). Histograms and filtering.
  a) Draw the histogram of the left and right figure (2x2
     checkerboard vs 10x10 checkerboard), with axis values.
  b) Give the 5x5 averaging/mean mask: all entries 1/25 = 0.0400.
  c) Draw the histogram after filtering the LEFT image with the mean
     mask, zero-padded edges; provide x and y values. The answer
     works through the overlaps: first overlap 200*5 + 20*20 = 1400,
     /25 = 56; second 2300/25 = 92; third 3200/25 = 128; fourth
     4100/25 = 164; frequency = (350*4) - (2*4) = 1392 pixels.
  d) Same for the RIGHT image: centres 78, 99, 106, 114, 121, 142
     with frequencies 162, 324, 162, 162, 324, 162; frequency total
     12600 - 648 = 11952 pixels. (The answer text has some internal
     inconsistency in the exact overlap arithmetic, which is itself a
     study warning: know the METHOD, not the exact number.)
  Teaching point: this is the hardest question type — you must be able
  to (i) construct a normalized averaging mask, (ii) reason about how
  convolution with zero padding changes boundary pixel values,
  (iii) count how many output pixels take each value, and (iv) draw
  the resulting histogram. It combines lesson 4 (convolution), lesson
  6 (histograms) and lesson 7 (mean filter).

Overall exam reading:
 - 90 minutes, 7 questions, mix of calculation, MCQ, code reading,
   and one heavy histogram/convolution question.
 - Heavily weighted to lessons 1, 2, 4, 6, 7. Notably light on
   morphology, edge detection, segmentation, features, pattern
   recognition in this particular sample — BUT the webpage lists
   separate sample exams for period 1 and period 2, so this sample is
   period 1 and likely covers the earlier material. Period 2 sample
   exams (which I do not have the content of) probably cover lessons
   8-14: frequency filtering, restoration, morphology, edges,
   segmentation, features, pattern recognition.
 - Therefore: do not infer from this one sample that morphology etc.
   is unimportant. Infer that period 1 is early-material-heavy.

The recurring MATLAB traps the course deliberately teaches:
 1-based vs 0-based indexing (I+1 for LUT/transformation functions).
 uint8 saturation and truncation of negatives (Laplacian, subtraction).
 Class mismatch in arithmetic (convert to double first).
 imshow needs [] when data is outside [0,1] or [0,255].
 fftshift before displaying any spectrum or distance matrix.
 real() after ifft2.
 Elementwise (.*, .^, ./) vs matrix (*, ^, /).
 .^ vs ^ in vectorized loops.
 Preallocation and vectorization for speed.
 Semicolon suppresses output; %{ %} for block comments; %% for cells.

  -> Evaluate (confusion matrix, accuracy, sensitivity, precision,
     recall, F1, ROC)

14. MY READING OF THE MODULE'S CHARACTER AND WHAT MATTERS
================================================================

1. It is a CLASSICAL image processing course, not a deep learning
   course. The minor's title oversells the ML/DL content. The
   examinable ML is kNN plus a toy perceptron. If I want the DL part
   of the minor, it comes from the optional MATLAB Academy courses,
   not from this module's assessment.

2. It is heavily MATLAB-practical. Almost every concept arrives with
   a function and a snippet. The exam, however, tests understanding
   WITHOUT the IDE: hand calculations, hand convolution, hand
   bitwise ops, hand path finding, code reading. So I need to be able
   to do by hand what MATLAB does for me.

3. The lecturer reuses a small, fixed image set. Learning what
   coins.png, eight.tif, cameraman.tif, moon.tif, blobs.png, pout.tif,
   mandrill.tif and the apples/bananas set look like and behave like
   makes the answer PDFs far easier to follow, because the answers
   constantly refer to "the peak on the left", "the brightened image",
   "the coin edges" etc.

4. The answer PDFs are the highest-value study artifact after the
   decks. They contain the exact phrasing, the exact numbers, and
   the exact reasoning the lecturer expects. Where a deck is vague,
   the answer PDF pins it down.

5. The frequency-domain material (lesson 8, ch 14) and fddemo.m are
   the best-worked example of "theory -> code" in the whole module:
   distmatrix builds the distance matrix, generate_filter writes each
   H, Update does multiply-inverse-transform. If I understand that
   one script, I understand every filter equation in the course.

6. The histogram/convolution question (sample exam Q7) is the highest
   difficulty-per-mark item I have seen. It requires combining
   masking, zero-padded convolution, value counting and histogram
   construction. This is where I should invest the most practice.

7. The hardware/illumination material (lesson 2, ch 4) looks soft but
   is directly examined (sample exam Q2, Q3) as MCQ and classification
   from diagrams. The four-cornerstone framework (geometry, pattern,
   wavelength, filters) and the four techniques (back, diffuse,
   partial bright field/directional, dark field) are the answer
   vocabulary. The checklist reinforces exactly this.

8. The lens calculation (sample exam Q1) is a reliable mark: learn
   magnification = sensor size / FOV, focal length = m*WD/(1+m), and
   h'/s' = h/s, plus the line-pairs conversion (elements/mm divided
   by 2). Also learn the "sensor size nomenclature is legacy, no
   direct relation to dimensions" fact, since it explains why a 2/3"
   sensor is 8.8 x 6.6 mm and a 1/3" sensor is 4.8 x 3.6 mm.

9. There is a period 1 / period 2 split in the sample exams on the
   webpage. The one I have is period 1 and it is early-material
   heavy (lessons 1-7). I should expect a period 2 sample to be
   heavy on lessons 8-14: frequency filtering, restoration,
   morphology, edge detection, segmentation, feature extraction,
   pattern recognition. Do not under-study those because the period 1
   sample ignores them.

10. The assignments (after lesson 7 and after lesson 14) are project/
    presentation based. The webpage's "Training Image Labeler",
    "Image Region Analyzer", "Image Segmenter", "Color Thresholder"
    apps and the apple/banana classification pipeline in ch 20 are
    almost certainly the template for those assignments. The ch 20
    pipeline (read images -> grayscale -> average filter -> threshold
    -> complement -> fill holes -> bwlabel -> regionprops -> thinness
    ratio + eccentricity -> fitcknn -> confusionmat) is the module's
    end-to-end demonstration and probably the assignment's shape.

11. Deep learning hooks exist in the webpage but nothing in the exam
    or syllabus. If the minor requires DL output, it will come from
    the online courses or a separate module, not from IPF's written
    exam.

12. The module rewards precision and punishes hand-waving. The answer
    PDFs are blunt and numeric: "1/9", "0.4941", "-0.6588", "2.7",
    "1392 pixels", "3.7 line pairs/mm". The exam will likely want
    the same bluntness.
